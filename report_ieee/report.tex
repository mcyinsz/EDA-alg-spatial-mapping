\title{DNN Mapping Optimization on Spatial Accelerators:\\
Problem Formulation and Algorithm Comparison}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Course Project: Integrated Circuit Engineering Algorithms\\
Code: \url{https://github.com/mcyinsz/EDA-alg-spatial-mapping}}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Spatial accelerators (e.g., Cerebras WSE, Groq TSP) interconnect thousands of compute-memory cores in a 2D mesh. Deploying a DNN requires solving a coupled \emph{partitioning} (cores per layer) and \emph{placement} (physical mesh positions) problem whose search space grows combinatorially. We formulate inference-latency minimization with an analytical cost model covering compute parallelism, inter-layer communication, and intra-layer tensor-parallel synchronization. Four solvers---integer linear programming (ILP, used as a placement reference), simulated annealing (SA), a nested evolution strategy (EA), and Greedy+Kernighan-Lin (Greedy+KL)---are compared on 19 feasible configurations (7 workloads $\times$ 3 mesh sizes). After correcting the experimental setup so that solvers may leave cores idle and use cost-aware partitioning, Greedy+KL wins 12/19, SA 6/19, and EA 1/19 under the main model. An inter-only ablation yields SA 8/19 and Greedy+KL 8/19; a $\kappa$ sweep and an intra-parallel ablation further reshuffle the ranking (EA reaching 9/19). The central finding is that \textbf{modeling assumptions and experimental setup jointly and decisively determine algorithm rankings}, a caution for analytical mapping studies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Spatial architectures place compute and memory together on hundreds to millions of cores connected by a 2D mesh network-on-chip (NoC). Representative platforms include the Cerebras WSE-3 ($\sim$900k cores)~\cite{lie2024}, the Groq TSP~\cite{abts2020}, and Intel Loihi~2. On such hardware, inference latency depends not only on \emph{what} model runs but on \emph{how} it is mapped: a poor mapping causes either load imbalance (compute bottleneck) or NoC congestion (communication bottleneck)~\cite{yik2025,timcheck2026}.

The mapping problem couples two sub-problems. \textbf{Partitioning} decides how many cores each layer receives, trading parallelism for communication volume. \textbf{Placement} decides where those cores sit, affecting Manhattan routing distance. The two are tightly coupled: changing the partition alters the optimal placement and vice versa. For a 10-layer network on a 152-core chip, partitioning alone exceeds $10^{13}$ choices and placement exceeds $10^{267}$~\cite{wever2026}.

Prior mapping tools such as Timeloop~\cite{parashar2019}, GAMMA~\cite{kao2020}, and DiGAMMA~\cite{kao2022} target hierarchical-memory accelerators and model tiling and dataflow ordering rather than NoC routing distance and physical placement. Wever et al.~\cite{wever2026} introduce evolutionary mapping with hardware-in-the-loop on Loihi~2, but require $\sim$5\,s per evaluation on a single 152-core chip. We instead study a general analytical formulation and contribute: (1)~a unified three-term cost model for heterogeneous (linear and convolutional) layers; (2)~four solvers in a common framework with a corrected, fair setup (idle cores allowed, aligned budgets, ILP repositioned as a placement reference); and (3)~systematic ablations ($\kappa$ sweep, intra-parallel assumption, inter-only) showing that modeling choices dominate the observed algorithm ranking.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\subsection{Model and Variables}
A workload has $L$ layers; layer $i$ is described by FLOPs $F_i$, weight bytes $W_i$, and output-activation bytes $A_i$. For a linear layer with input/output dims $n_{i-1},n_i$ and sparsity $s_i$: $F_i=2n_{i-1}n_i(1{-}s_i)$, $W_i=4n_{i-1}n_i(1{-}s_i)$, $A_i=4n_i$; convolutional layers use the analogous $C_{\text{in}},C_{\text{out}},k,H_{\text{out}},W_{\text{out}}$ expressions. The accelerator is an $H{\times}W$ mesh of $K{=}HW$ homogeneous cores with per-core memory $M$, throughput $P$ (FLOP/cycle), and link cost $\beta$ (cycles per byte-hop). The decision variables are the partition $\mathbf{x}=(x_1,\dots,x_L)\in\mathbb{Z}_+^L$ and the placement sets $S_i\subset\{(r,c)\}$ with $|S_i|=x_i$.

\subsection{Objective}
We minimize single-inference latency (in cycles):
\begin{equation}
\min_{\mathbf{x},\{S_i\}} T = \sum_{i=1}^{L} T_{\text{comp},i} + \sum_{i=1}^{L-1} T_{\text{inter},i} + \sum_{i=1}^{L} T_{\text{intra},i}
\label{eq:obj}
\end{equation}
with the three terms
\begin{align}
T_{\text{comp},i} &= \frac{F_i}{x_i P}, \qquad
T_{\text{inter},i} = \beta\,\frac{A_i}{x_i}\,\bar{d}_{\text{inter}}(S_i,S_{i+1}), \\
T_{\text{intra},i} &= \beta\,\kappa_i\,A_i\Big(1-\tfrac{1}{x_i}\Big)\,\bar{d}_{\text{intra}}(S_i)\quad(x_i{>}1),
\label{eq:intra}
\end{align}
where $\bar{d}_{\text{inter}}$ and $\bar{d}_{\text{intra}}$ are the average pairwise Manhattan distances between and within layer core sets, and $\kappa_i$ is the tensor-parallel factor ($\kappa_i{=}1.0$ for linear, $0.5$ for convolution). Equation~\eqref{eq:intra} models a simplified 1-D tensor-parallel synchronization: each of $x_i$ cores holds an $A_i/x_i$ shard and exchanges data with the others, giving volume of order $A_i(1{-}1/x_i)$.

\subsection{Constraints and Properties}
(C1)~$S_i\cap S_j=\emptyset$ for $i{\neq}j$; (C2)~$(W_i{+}A_i)/x_i\le M$; (C3)~$x_i\ge1$; and $\sum_i x_i \le K$, where \emph{idle cores are explicitly allowed} ($\sum_i x_i$ may be strictly less than $K$). From (C2), $x_i^{\min}=\lceil(W_i{+}A_i)/M\rceil$. The objective is non-convex (piecewise-constant distance terms), tightly coupled ($\mathbf{x}$ appears in both numerator and denominator), and combinatorially explosive.

\section{Algorithms}
\textbf{ILP (placement reference).} Binary $z_{i,k}$ assign core $k$ to layer $i$; quadratic distance terms are linearized with auxiliary variables $u_{i,k,l}$ (inter) and $w_{i,k,l}$ (intra), totaling $O(K^2L)$. Because this scale is prohibitive, the ILP fixes a greedy partition and \emph{optimizes placement only}. We therefore treat it as a reference for the placement sub-problem rather than a joint optimizer; it runs with a 60\,s limit on $\le16$-core meshes (PuLP+CBC).

\textbf{Simulated Annealing (SA).} State $(\mathbf{x},\{S_i\})$. The partition neighborhood now offers three moves---\emph{transfer} a core between layers, \emph{discard} a core (it becomes idle), and \emph{enable} an idle core---and the placement neighborhood swaps $\lfloor\sqrt{K_{\text{used}}}\rfloor$ core pairs. The initial state uses the minimum per-layer cores (no forced filling). Metropolis acceptance with $T_0{=}100$, $\gamma{=}0.995$, 3000 iterations, 5 runs.

\textbf{Nested Evolution Strategy (EA).} Following~\cite{wever2026}: an outer ES evolves a partition genotype $(x_i^{\text{extra}},C_{\text{unused}})$ and an inner ES evolves a placement permutation, with a reordering operator that preserves spatial locality when the partition changes. $(1{+}\lambda)$ selection, $\lambda{=}4$, 40 generations, 5 runs.

\textbf{Greedy+KL.} (1)~cost-aware greedy partitioning that adds a core only when total latency (including communication) decreases; (2)~column-major contiguous placement followed by Kernighan-Lin pair swaps; (3)~partition refinement that may move \emph{or remove} a core. Deterministic, one run.

\section{Experimental Setup}
Tables~\ref{tab:work} and~\ref{tab:solver} list the seven workloads and solver parameters. All meshes use $M{=}512$\,KB, $P{=}64$\,FLOP/cycle, $\beta{=}1.0$ at 1\,GHz; latencies are in $\mu$s and only meaningful relatively. Large-MLP and Transformer-L exceed the 16 cores of the 4$\times$4 mesh, leaving 19 feasible configurations. Five baselines (random, packed row/col-major, spread, equal-partition) are included. We run three ablations: \texttt{--inter-only} (drop $T_{\text{intra}}$), \texttt{--intra-parallel} (divide $T_{\text{intra}}$ by $x_i$, matching the inter-layer parallel-injection assumption), and a global $\kappa$ sweep (\texttt{scripts/sweep\_kappa.py}). All solver outputs are re-verified by recomputing latency from the stored placements (928/928 checks pass).

The Small-MLP result is the clearest effect of the setup fix: by keeping cores idle, SA avoids the spurious intra-layer cost that previously made optimizers \emph{lose} to a trivial packed baseline.

\subsection{Why Greedy+KL Leads on Large Workloads}
Greedy+KL's contiguous, column-major placement keeps intra-layer average distance near 1--2 hops, whereas SA's random swaps and EA's mutations fragment a layer's cores (3--5 hops). On the 8$\times$8 mesh, where distances reach 14 hops, this gap widens: on Transformer-L/8$\times$8 Greedy+KL (54.4\,$\mu$s) beats SA (68.2) by 20\% and EA (74.6) by 27\%. The compute-/inter-/intra-layer breakdown (Fig.~\ref{fig:breakdown}) confirms Greedy+KL keeps the intra-layer share lowest. Its cost-aware partitioning also avoids over-provisioning on tiny workloads, fixing the previous degradation where forced core-filling inflated communication.

\subsection{Where the Heuristics Differ}
SA's idle-core moves dominate Small-MLP (3.20 vs.\ Greedy 3.46\,$\mu$s) and remain competitive on the compute-bound ConvNet/8$\times$8. EA is limited by its small evaluation budget ($\sim$322 evals/run, an order of magnitude below SA), winning only Transformer-S/4$\times$4 in the main model. Run-to-run variation is modest for the deterministic regimes but appreciable for ConvNet/6$\times$6 (SA mean $1132\pm189\,\mu$s) where the random-swap neighborhood struggles, reinforcing that the best-of-5 numbers favor the stochastic solvers.

\subsection{Ablation 1: Inter-only Model}
Removing the intra-layer term re-balances the ranking to SA~8, Greedy+KL~8, EA~3. Without a compactness incentive, Greedy+KL's KL swaps can fragment layers badly (ConvNet/8$\times$8 1005\,$\mu$s), while EA wins all three Transformer-S meshes.

\subsection{Ablations and Communication Sensitivity}
\textbf{Intra-parallel assumption.} Dividing $T_{\text{intra}}$ by $x_i$ shifts the ranking to EA~9, SA~7, Greedy+KL~3, removing Greedy+KL's compactness advantage. \textbf{$\kappa$ sweep.} Table~\ref{tab:kappa} and Fig.~\ref{fig:kappa} show solver wins vs.\ a global $\kappa$ multiplier: at $\kappa{=}0$ SA and Greedy+KL tie (8 each); EA peaks at $\kappa{=}0.25$ (11); Greedy+KL leads for $\kappa{\ge}0.5$ (9--12). Three internally consistent models (main, inter-only, intra-parallel) each crown a different winner.

\textbf{Communication cost and core utilization.} Sweeping $\beta$ (Table~\ref{tab:comm}, Fig.~\ref{fig:kappa}) shows Greedy+KL fills 64/64 cores when comm is cheap ($\kappa{=}0$, $\beta{\le}0.1$) but uses only 24/64 at $\kappa{=}\beta{=}1.0$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:mapping}); equal 64-core partitioning at default $\kappa$ is slower (49.5 vs.\ 42.7\,$\mu$s).

\section{Conclusion}
We compared four mapping solvers under a corrected setup where idle cores are allowed. Rankings are not robust to the cost model: Greedy+KL leads the main model (12/19), while inter-only, intra-parallel, and $\kappa$ sweeps each crown a different winner. \textbf{Limitations}: serial execution, inconsistent inter/intra parallelism, linear-chain Transformers, assumed $\kappa,\beta$. \textbf{Future work}: compactness-biased neighborhoods for SA/EA, pipelined objectives, and hardware validation.
